# Consistent second-person narration — proposal only

This text is **not recorded** beyond the opening. It preserves the sixteen-part research story and the supplied conclusions. Timing and final wording remain subject to voice/opening approval. No claim of a completed three-minute cut is made.

1. **Notebook.** You're in maths class. The front of your notebook has equations. The back? Your IPL eleven. And let's just say... one side gets a lot more attention.
2. **Opportunity.** Years pass. The cricket notebook survives. Then you see the Rajasthan Royals SupeRR Selector challenge. Your imaginary selections suddenly need something rather inconvenient. Evidence.
3. **Cleaning.** Your first obstacle: two hundred thousand, nine hundred and ninety-nine delivery events. Wides. No-balls. Run-outs. Rain. Before you judge a player, you have to reconstruct the match correctly.
4. **Before the ball.** So you freeze time before every ball. Score. Wickets. Balls remaining. No peeking at the six that happens next. That would be cheating, with a spreadsheet.
5. **Difficulty versus consequence.** But difficult doesn't always mean consequential. Thirty-nine runs an over? Terrifying. Also, probably over. Two needed off one? Now the next ball can change everything.
6. **Pressure.** You combine scoreboard difficulty with win-probability leverage, using context-relative percentiles. Your primary pressure definition selects the top twenty percent within each innings. Not within every phase.
7. **Expectation.** Next question: better than what? You estimate expected runs and dismissal risk. Batters earn credit for extra runs and survival. Bowlers, for runs prevented and credited wickets.
8. **Context and assumptions.** You adjust for scoring eras and opposition. Then combine runs and wickets with a declared assumption: fifteen runs per wicket. You check ten and twenty too. An assumption deserves a test, not a disguise.
9. **Shrinkage.** Then the small samples arrive, wearing enormous confidence. Twenty-five pressure events are not four hundred. Empirical Bayes pulls weakly supported estimates toward the average.
10. **Quality versus delta.** Performing well under pressure isn't the same as improving because of pressure. You find no validated repeatable bowling pressure delta. The batting delta gains are tiny. A psychological clutch trait remains unproven.
11. **Holdout.** The unseen twenty-twenty-six season challenges your story. Batting quality shows no positive predictive relationship. Bowling looks promising, but an advantage over ordinary economy isn't established.
12. **Uncertainty.** So you resample whole matches, seven hundred and fifty times. Confidence intervals. Rank distributions. A top-ten bootstrap rate measures conditional historical stability. It isn't a prophecy.
13. **Sensitivity.** Change the pressure definition, and your shortlist changes. Remove a player's best season, and nine names leave each top ten. Those aren't details you get to hide.
14. **Shortlist.** Your primary lists put Jos Buttler and Jasprit Bumrah first. But every name needs exposure, uncertainty and format mix. T-twenty international evidence isn't automatically IPL evidence.
15. **Reproducibility.** Finally, the report's replay reproduces all twenty rankings and passes forty-one checks. The code repeats. The statistical limitations stay. Those are the supplied report's results; this film doesn't rerun the private analysis.
16. **Return to the notebook.** Your notebook used to ask, who would you pick? Khiladi asks a better question. What does the evidence support... and how sure should you be?
